# Target-driven design — why the target audience gets fixed first

The same service has a different correct design once the target audience changes. Color, type
size, tone, and visual style all ripple out from the target, so the brief's ② target audience must
be settled before moving on to ③ and beyond.

## How to write the target audience

Age and gender alone aren't enough. Write these three elements as one set.

- Persona: age range, occupation, comfort level with technology, etc.
- Situation: what moment are they in when they encounter this service
- Emotion: what are they feeling in that situation, and what do they need

"Women in their 30s" alone decides nothing. Write "a freelancer in her early 30s, pressed for time
by deadlines, who wants a simple, reassuring UI over anything complicated to operate" — only then
does it become material for deciding color and type size.

## Example: the same service, three different targets

Using a language-learning app as an example, here's how the correct design shifts across targets.

| Dimension | Older adults | Women in their 20s | Corporate training managers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Color | Strong contrast, calm navy base | Warm coral pink, soft palette | White background, trust-oriented navy/blue |
| Type size | Body text 18px or larger, bolder typeface | Body text 16px, rounded and approachable typeface | Body text 15-16px, highly legible sans-serif |
| Tone | Clarity and reassurance above all | Approachability, the fun of a change of pace | Efficiency, visible results |
| Style | Flat design, minimal decoration | Claymorphism leaning pop | Corporate × dashboard UI |
| Impression to avoid | Information overload, complicated operation | A dry, businesslike feel | Too casual, overdoing the playfulness |

The same "language learning" service pulls in clearly different directions: reducing confusion for
older adults, communicating fun for women in their 20s, and conveying results and trust for
corporate training managers.

## How the target ripples into each dimension

Once the target is fixed, apply it across dimensions with this correspondence.

- Older or less tech-comfortable audience → larger type, simpler operation, stronger contrast
- Younger audience wanting a soft impression → rounded typefaces, a pastel-leaning palette,
  claymorphism or Kawaii-style visuals
- Corporate decision-makers → a trust-oriented palette (navy/blue), a dashboard-leaning layout
  that surfaces data and results

## Always pair with the impression to avoid

Stating only the impression you want tends to pull an AI or designer toward something safe and
generic. Pairing it — "make it modern, but avoid anything that feels cold" — sharpens the result.
